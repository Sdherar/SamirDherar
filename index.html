path-loss analyzer

Developed RF detection and power-distribution hardware for a 10-channel, Qualcomm-sponsored path-loss measurement system. Designed the detector PCB and implemented frequency-dependent calibration.

Inside the project +

Hardware ownership

Designed the RF detector and power-distribution PCBs, including ten receive channels and controlled-impedance routing. Integrated the multi-channel measurement architecture and contributed to the RF switch board and mechanical integration.

Calibration

Applied six known power levels at multiple RF frequencies and compared detector readings against a reference power meter. A quadratic calibration function incorporating voltage, frequency, and cross terms converted detector voltage to RF input power.

The résumé reports approximately 0.1 dB calibration accuracy. Raw calibration data and the measurement uncertainty budget are not included here; this is not a full-band accuracy guarantee.

Engineering tradeoffs

A buck stage converted the 12 V supply to approximately 6 V for the synthesizer, followed by local regulation where needed. At 1 A, a direct 12-to-6 V linear drop would dissipate approximately 6 W, making the switching stage the more practical thermal choice.

Debugging

  1. Observation: Broadband detector readings were biased below approximately 1 GHz.
  2. Investigation: Examined the RF source’s spectral content.
  3. Root cause: Source harmonics contributed power beyond the intended fundamental.
  4. Resolution: Moved to a cleaner RF source.

High-Speed Differential Via & Stackup Optimization

Investigated controlled-impedance routing and differential-via transitions on a 24-layer PCB stackup using HFSS. Evaluated via stubs, backdrilling, and return-path geometry against insertion loss and simulated eye performance.

Analysis scope +

Impedance planning

Compared eight candidate layers for 35, 50, and 65 Ω single-ended routes and 80, 100, and 110 Ω differential pairs. Screened the nominal geometries against an assumed 3.5 mil trace/space floor, with fabrication tolerances still requiring confirmation.

Initial problem and design improvement

The baseline model showed a differential insertion-loss notch at 4.84 GHz. Backdrilling removed the dominant stub resonance; return-path vias and antipad tuning formed the next optimization steps. The unused via barrel acts as a resonant stub; removing it reduces the notch that limits channel transmission.

System-level simulation result

Backdrilling reopened the simulated 10 Gb/s eye. The report shows a usable 25 Gb/s eye in the current model; 30 Gb/s remains exploratory pending wider-band EM analysis and tolerance checks.

Team simulation study with Pavan and Vaishnav. Nominal modeled performance, not measured board validation.

High-Speed Interconnect Characterization

Measured cable propagation, impedance discontinuities, transmission-line reflections, and PCB terminations using VNA and TDR techniques. Correlated laboratory measurements with ADS circuit models.

Results and measurement lessons +

Termination geometry

Measured S11 from 6 MHz to 6 GHz. The SMD termination maintained S11 below −10 dB to 2.68 GHz, versus 1.42 GHz for the axial implementation. Fitted effective series inductance was 1.4 nH versus 3.9 nH, including the mounting and local return path.

The fixture changes the result

Turning PTFE microstrip traces away from the desk raised measured impedance by 4.27 Ω and 6.41 Ω for the two tested traces. The setup’s surroundings mattered more than the differences between the field solvers.

Cable delay and model limits

Measured 1.587 ns/ft against a nominal 1.540 ns/ft, about 3% higher. Comparing 6-inch and 3-foot cable transients showed that a small load-side capacitance improved early alignment, while additional short-cable damping remained an effective fit with an unresolved physical source.

Team lab reports: Labs 1.1–1.3 with Elior Bilow; Labs 2.2–2.3 with Anthony Hamamji. These are measured laboratory results and fitted models.

RF LABORATORIES

RF & Microwave Characterization

Hands-on characterization of RF and microwave systems spanning approximately 100 MHz–12 GHz. Investigated impedance matching, microwave power detection, antenna patterns, transistor amplifiers, heterodyne receivers, and FMCW/Doppler radar.

9 GHz power calibration20–21 dB RF gainFMCW / Doppler radar
Explore the measurements +

Microwave power and detector characterization

Investigated 9 GHz power measurements, thermistor-based DC-substitution calibration, detector input matching, and diode-detector behavior. The recorded thermistor result reached approximately −22.7 dB S11 at 9 GHz under the lowest-resistance condition tested; this is an input-match result, not an absolute power-accuracy specification.

Antenna characterization

Measured X-band horn E- and H-plane radiation patterns with approximately 36° and 40° beamwidths, respectively. The recorded 90° polarization rotation reduced received power by approximately 24 dB; this single observation is not a fully characterized cross-polarization isolation specification. Also investigated parabolic reflectors and antenna matching with a 2–13 GHz S11 sweep.

RF amplifier measurements

Characterized microstrip FET amplifiers, bias networks, and bias tees through small-signal, large-signal, and two-tone intermodulation experiments. Observed approximately 20–21 dB power gain across the recorded input sweep. No verified P1dB or IP3 value is claimed.

Heterodyne communications

Configured an approximately 9 GHz RF carrier and 9.03 GHz local oscillator to produce a 30 MHz intermediate frequency. The receive chain used horn antennas, a mixer, and IF observation with a spectrum analyzer and oscilloscope; laboratory work included AM demodulation and IF response to RF/LO power.

FMCW and Doppler radar

Used an 8–9 GHz FMCW sweep over 20 ms, measuring beat frequency and accounting for internal propagation delay before calculating range. One recorded example at a nominal 0.40 m target distance recalculates to approximately 0.426 m after calibration; this is an individual example, not a system-wide accuracy specification.

The plotted stationary-target sweep covers 45 cm to 1 m, with beat frequencies of approximately 476–714 Hz and scatter between readings. A separate Doppler experiment related X-band frequency shifts to radial target velocity using a waveguide tuner, diode detector, and horn antenna.

ECEN 4634/5634 microwave laboratory. The ranging plot preserves recorded values, including repeated readings; it is not a fitted accuracy or resolution claim.

ALLYE / GRADUATE PROJECT

60 GHz Handheld Radar Platform

Developing a battery-powered 60 GHz radar platform integrating motion sensing, Bluetooth connectivity, inertial sensing, and a low-power display. Focused on hardware integration, PCB design, and system-level power management.

Embedded hardware · Power budgeting · PCB design
Platform and power budget +
60 GHzRadar sensing

System architecture

My work focuses on the hardware and PCB: power architecture, schematic integration, component placement, and accessible programming and measurement interfaces. The platform combines radar processing with a wireless MCU, inertial sensing, and a memory LCD; the radar SPI interface is planned for up to 15 MHz.

Power architecture

A protected single-cell 18650 battery provides approximately 11.70 Wh based on minimum rated capacity. The calculated maximum battery demand is 1.83 A, or 0.563C. A regulated 3.3 V rail supplies the main electronics, with battery-voltage supervision intended to shut the rail down before the cell protection cutoff.

The two-panel solar configuration is estimated to supply approximately 80 mA of battery charge current in strong sunlight, below the harvesting stage’s 100 mA limit. Solar input supplements the battery; recharge and runtime depend on light conditions and operating duty cycle.

PCB integration and current status

Initial full component placement and a further schematic review were completed by October 3. A custom radar breakout supports interface validation. Test access covers power rails and current sensing, programming/reset, radar SPI/control, and the IMU/display interfaces.

Next steps are power verification, protection thresholds, interface ESD protection, wireless matching/antenna completion, and critical routing. Target-detection performance and battery runtime remain to be measured.

Work in progress · Power values are design calculations and estimates, not validated field performance.
